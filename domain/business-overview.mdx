The milk distribution business sits in the middle of the dairy supply chain: a company called the **distributor** buys dairy products from one or more dairies every day and delivers those products to retail clients — shops, hotels, canteens, and similar customers — by early morning the following day. Because dairy products are perishable, this cycle repeats every single day without exception, and virtually every business operation must complete within a 24-hour window. The ERP system you are working with exists to coordinate every step of that cycle, from capturing client orders the night before, through dispatching vehicles, to reconciling cash at the end of the day.

## Who Buys from Whom

At the top of the chain sit **dairies** — manufacturing facilities like Govind Dairy or Shakti Dairy that produce milk, curd, lassi, and buttermilk under named **brands**. The distributor places a daily purchase order with these dairies and collects the goods at their factory gates. The physical document the dairy issues at collection time is called a **gatepass**; the date printed on it follows a per-brand policy (either the same day or the previous calendar day).

The distributor is not a single monolithic entity in this system. Multiple distributor accounts can coexist, each handling a specific product category or a specific set of delivery routes. For example, Distributor A might procure and supply all milk products to Groups 1–9, Distributor B might supply milk to Group 10, and Distributor C might supply all non-milk products (curd, lassi, buttermilk) to every group. This multi-distributor design means the system must track which distributor is responsible for each product on each route.

## Who Sells to Whom

The distributor sells to **clients** — retail outlets that place recurring daily orders. Each client is assigned to a **delivery group** (the route and vehicle that physically drops off the goods) and a **billing group** (the route used for billing and collections). In straightforward cases, both groups are the same. Each client is also "owned" by a specific distributor, which determines which price list applies when calculating bills.

## The Daily Cycle

<Steps>
  <Step title="Evening: Order Entry (Night Shift)">
    The evening before a sale date, employees open the day's **Order Paper** and enter how many units each client has ordered for each product. These are called **night entries**. Because the paper is still in `DRAFT` status, quantities can be freely edited until the paper is submitted.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Night: Night Submission">
    Once all night orders are captured, an employee submits the night entries. The paper moves to `NIGHT_SUBMITTED` status. At this point, vehicle allocations for the night delivery session are locked in, and route cash settlements become available for entry.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Early Morning: Delivery and Morning Entries">
    Vehicles depart overnight and deliver products. In the morning, employees record the **actual quantities delivered** (which may differ from what was ordered, for example if a client was closed or stock ran short). These are called **morning entries**. The system calculates the final bill amount for each client at this stage, incorporating GST where applicable.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Morning: Morning Submission">
    Once all delivered quantities are recorded, the paper is submitted again to move to `MORNING_SUBMITTED` status. The system automatically generates any **distributor transfer** records needed to account for products that one distributor physically supplied on behalf of another.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Day: Finalization">
    An Admin reviews the completed paper, verifies collections, purchases, and tray movements, then **finalizes** the paper. Once finalized, the day's records are locked. If a correction is needed, the Admin can **reopen** the paper with a documented reason.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Products the Business Handles

All products fall into two broad supply categories: **MILK** and **NON\_MILK**. Milk includes cow milk, buffalo milk, toned milk, and full-cream milk. Non-milk includes curd, lassi, and buttermilk. This distinction matters because different distributors may handle each category, and the system enforces category-level supply rules per delivery group.

Every product is a specific SKU defined by its brand, product type, packaging type, and size — for example, "Govind Cow Milk 500 ml Pouch" or "Govind Sweet Lassi 200 ml Bottle". Products are linked to distributors through **product links**, and each product link carries its own purchase rate and selling rate history.

## Open Questions

The following questions are not answered in the current documentation and are worth clarifying before implementing or extending any part of this system.

- **How are client selling rates negotiated and updated?** The system supports scheduled rate changes with effective date ranges, but it is not clear who is authorized to create or modify a client's rate, and whether there is an approval workflow before a new rate takes effect.
- **What happens when a driver does not return all trays?** The tray tracking modules record closing balances, but it is unclear how unresolved tray shortages are escalated, charged back to clients, or reconciled at the end of a period.
- **Can a paper be reopened more than once?** The reopen workflow allows a finalized paper to be corrected and re-finalized. Whether this cycle (finalize → reopen → finalize) can repeat multiple times for the same paper, and whether there is any limit, is not documented.
- **What triggers the morning delivery session for a group?** The system distinguishes between NIGHT and MORNING delivery sessions, but the business rules for which groups use which session — and whether a group can switch sessions over time — are not explicitly defined.
- **How are inactive clients handled mid-cycle?** If a client is deactivated after a paper has already been generated and order sheets created, it is unclear whether that client's items are preserved, hidden, or need manual removal.
